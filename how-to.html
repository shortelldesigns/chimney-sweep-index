When to sweep a chimney

Two different jobs get bundled as “annual chimney cleaning.” One is an inspection. The other is a sweep (mechanical cleaning). Fire-safety organizations treat them separately. This page summarizes public consumer guidance from CSIA, NFPA, and the U.S. Fire Administration. It is not a substitute for NFPA 211, for a local fire code, or for a qualified inspection of your building.

Inspect every year. Sweep when deposits warrant it.

CSIA says it follows NFPA 211, which states that chimneys, fireplaces, and vents shall be inspected at least once a year for soundness, freedom from deposits, and correct clearances, and that cleaning, maintenance, and repairs shall be done if necessary.

CSIA’s homeowner FAQ is specific about the cleaning threshold:

  • Masonry fireplaces: CSIA recommends sweeping at 1/8 inch of sooty buildup, and sooner if any glaze (creosote) is present. CSIA describes that amount as enough buildup to cause a chimney fire capable of damaging the chimney or spreading to the home.
  • Factory-built fireplaces: CSIA recommends sweeping when any appreciable buildup occurs, because the deposit is acidic and can corrode prefabricated components.

Unused chimneys still get the annual inspection. CSIA notes that animals may nest in a flue, and other deterioration can make a chimney unsafe even if you barely burned.

Gas appliances are not exempt. CSIA’s FAQ: gas is generally a clean-burning fuel, but the chimney can still be blocked by nests or debris, and modern furnaces can have problems when vented through flues intended for older equipment. CSIA recommends annual inspections for all chimneys, gas furnaces included.

Sources: CSIA Homeowner Resources (FAQ: how often should I have my chimney swept?; I heat with gas); CSIA cites NFPA 211.

Why fall

NFPA’s consumer heating page notes that chimneys and heating systems should be inspected and cleaned each year before they are fired up for the season. Nearly half of home heating-equipment fires (46%) in NFPA’s 2020–2024 averages occurred from December through February.

The U.S. Fire Administration’s heating-fire message is the same in plainer words: maintain heating equipment and chimneys by having them cleaned and inspected each year by a professional. Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from fireplaces, wood stoves, and other heat sources.

Fall is the practical window: you still have time to book, and you are not lighting the first fire of the year on an uninspected flue.

Sources: NFPA Home Heating Safety; USFA Heating Fire Safety.

Inspection levels, in homeowner language

NFPA 211 defines three inspection levels. CSIA trains and tests certified sweeps against that framework. A technician who cannot explain the level they are offering is not a good match. This is a summary, not the standard.

  • Level 1 — CSIA describes this as the usual level for routine cleanings and annual inspections, when the chimney will continue to be used as it has been. It covers readily accessible portions of the appliance and chimney. It does not require removing building finishes.
  • Level 2 — Used when something has changed or an event has occurred: a property sale or transfer, a change of fuel or connected appliance, or after a chimney fire or other event that may have affected the system. It includes the Level 1 work plus accessible areas of the chimney structure and typically a video scan of the flue interior. A general home inspection is not automatically a Level 2 chimney inspection.
  • Level 3 — Reserved for suspected concealed damage, when a deeper look may require removing parts of the chimney or adjacent building components. It is not a routine upsell.

Ask which NFPA 211 level you are buying, what will be written down, and whether a camera scan is included. CSIA’s homeowner FAQ notes that a complete sweeping includes the chimney flue and smoke chamber — not only the firebox brick you can see from the room.

Sources: CSIA Homeowner Resources; CSIA Level 1 masonry-chimney inspection notes citing NFPA 211.

Chemical cleaners are not a sweep

CSIA does not treat fireplace “logs” or catalytic powders as a substitute for mechanical sweeping and inspection. Some professionals use chemistry to change glazed creosote so it can be brushed out. Debris still has to leave the flue, smoke shelf, and offsets. An annual inspection is still the way hidden structural problems show up.

Source: CSIA Homeowner Resources (Can I use chemical chimney cleaners?).

What to burn

CSIA’s homeowner FAQ: only well-seasoned wood. Unseasoned wood keeps smoke cooler and encourages creosote. Open the damper fully before lighting; CSIA recommends looking up with a flashlight first.

How to hire without a name on this site

  1. Search CSIA’s public finder by ZIP. Confirm the technician’s name, credential, and expiration on that site — certification is held by the person, not the truck.
  2. Check the state or local contractor lookup on this index’s state pages if the work includes repairs, caps, liners, or other home improvement, or if your state requires registration above a dollar threshold.
  3. Ask for a written report, the inspection level, and whether sweeping was actually needed under the 1/8-inch / glaze guidance.
  4. Insurance and a current credential card are reasonable questions. This site does not rank companies.

NCSG membership and NFI hearth credentials are additional public finders. They are not the same as CSIA certification. Verify each on its own site.

Carbon monoxide alarms are not a chimney inspection

USFA’s heating-fire message: keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from fireplaces and wood stoves, and have chimneys cleaned and inspected before the season. A working carbon monoxide alarm is extra protection around fuel-burning appliances. It does not tell you whether the flue is blocked or glazed. (paid link)
