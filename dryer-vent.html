Dryer exhaust

Dryer vents are not chimneys

A chimney carries combustion products from a fireplace, stove, or furnace. A clothes-dryer exhaust duct carries warm, moist air and lint from a laundry appliance to the outdoors. They are different systems, with different fuels and different credentials. Some chimney companies clean both. That does not make them the same job.

Do not exhaust a dryer into a chimney

Model mechanical codes used in many U.S. jurisdictions (the International Mechanical Code’s clothes-dryer exhaust section is the usual template) say dryer exhaust systems shall be independent of all other systems, shall convey moisture and combustion products to the outdoors, and shall not be connected to a vent connector, vent, or chimney. Whether that language is adopted in your town is a question for the local building or fire official — this page is not a code ruling on a specific house.

The practical homeowner point: a dryer pumping lint into a fireplace flue is the wrong pipe. If someone proposes that hookup, stop and get a second look.

Lint is the dryer analogue of creosote

NFPA’s consumer clothes-dryer tip sheet: the leading cause of home clothes dryer fires is failure to clean them. NFPA’s public advice includes:

  • Clean the lint filter before or after each load. Remove lint that has collected around the drum.
  • Use rigid or flexible metal venting to keep airflow and drying time reasonable. (Plastic foil accordion hose is a common weak point.)
  • Keep the exhaust pipe unrestricted and confirm the outdoor flap opens when the dryer runs.
  • Once a year — or sooner if clothes take longer than usual to dry — clean lint out of the vent pipe, or have a dryer lint-removal service do it.
  • Turn the dryer off if you leave home or go to bed.
  • Have gas dryers checked so the gas line and connection are intact.

Massachusetts Department of Fire Services, citing the same NFPA sheet, adds: an adult should be home when the dryer is running; clean outdoor vents twice a year; keep the area around the dryer clear.

NFPA research on home dryer fires (2014–2018 averages) estimated about 13,820 home structure fires per year in which dryers were involved in ignition, with failure to clean as a leading factor (about one-third) and dust, fiber, or lint a leading item first ignited.

Sources: NFPA Clothes Dryer Safety tip sheet; Massachusetts DFS Dryer Fire Safety; NFPA research brief Home Dryer Fires (Ahrens, 2020, 2014–2018 averages).

CSIA’s dryer credential is CDET, not CCS

CSIA Certified Chimney Sweep® (CCS®) is the chimney-and-venting exam. CSIA Certified Dryer Exhaust Technician® (CDET®) is a separate nationally offered credential for dryer vent cleaning and related work. A person may hold one, both, or neither. Verify the credential you were quoted on CSIA’s public finder.

NCSG and other trade groups may also list dryer-vent work. Membership is not the same as a CSIA CDET card. Check the card against the organization that issued it.

Source: CSIA Certified Dryer Exhaust Technician.

A lint kit is for the duct you can reach

NFPA’s consumer sheet says to clean lint out of the vent pipe once a year, or sooner if clothes take longer than usual to dry. A flexible-rod kit from the laundry room can clear an accessible run. It is not a substitute for a CSIA CDET (or equivalent) when the duct is long, crushed, or terminates where you cannot see the flap. (paid link)

Same visit, two invoices (sometimes)

Fall is a natural time to do both: chimney inspection before heating season, dryer-vent cleaning before winter restricts outdoor access to the termination. Ask the technician which system they are opening, which credential they hold for that system, and what they found. A clean lint screen does not mean the hidden duct is clear. A swept chimney does not mean the dryer line was touched.
